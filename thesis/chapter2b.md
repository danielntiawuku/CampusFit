## 2.7 Leaderboards, Competition and Social Comparison

The leaderboard is the most visible and most contested mechanic in the gamification canon. Its psychological basis is Festinger's (1954) social comparison theory, which proposes that people evaluate their own abilities and opinions by comparing themselves with others, and that comparison is especially frequent when objective evaluation is difficult. A fitness activity such as a walk to the library has no intrinsic scoreboard; a leaderboard supplies one.

Social comparison operates in two directions. **Upward comparison** (against those performing better) can motivate effort when the gap feels attainable and the cause of the gap is understood, and can demotivate when the gap feels permanent (Wood, 1989). **Downward comparison** protects self-esteem but rarely sustains effort. Gamification research has repeatedly observed that leaderboards improve aggregate performance while simultaneously increasing variance in experience: top-ranked users report higher engagement, and bottom-ranked users report lower engagement than they would in the absence of a ranking (Hamari et al., 2014; Sailer & Homner, 2020).

Design responses documented in the literature include percentile-based rather than absolute rankings, rotating leaderboards that reset periodically, team-based rather than individual competition, and opt-in visibility. CampusFit adopts three of these four: ranks are computed by a SQL view that returns a bounded window around the requesting student, the leaderboard is presented as a weekly league so that entry remains achievable, and clubs introduce a cooperative layer in which individuals contribute to a shared total. Privacy opt-in is handled by the profile visibility settings described in Section 4.8.

An important empirical nuance from Alzghoul et al. (2024) is that leaderboards appear to amplify the effect of points rather than substitute for them: the combination was significant where points alone were weaker. This supports the decision to implement all three point-badge-leaderboard mechanics as an interlocking system rather than to ship a points counter alone.

### 2.7.1 Design Patterns for Comparison Without Demotivation

The negative findings reviewed above have generated a small but consistent vocabulary of design responses, and each was evaluated for adoption here. **Percentile or windowed ranking** replaces absolute position, so that a student compares against a local reference class rather than against the head of the table. **Rotation** — resetting the ranking at a fixed interval — converts a permanent hierarchy into a recurring competition in which entry is always possible again. **Team-based competition** shifts the comparison target from individuals to groups, activating cooperative motives alongside competitive ones. **Opt-in visibility** lets a user withdraw from public ranking without withdrawing from the system. And **self-comparison** — performance against one's own history — provides a completely non-social reference standard.

CampusFit adopts windowed ranking, rotation, team-based competition, and self-comparison, and implements opt-in visibility partially through profile visibility settings. Windowing is realised structurally: the ranking view computes the full ordering inside the database but the interface renders a podium plus a bounded band of rows around the requesting student, with that student's own rank and score pinned at the top of the screen — comparison information without a full exposure of position. Rotation is realised as a weekly league framing, so a student who had a bad week competes again on equal terms on Monday rather than carrying a permanently visible deficit. Team competition is realised through clubs, whose shared totals let a student's individual contribution matter without requiring individual rank. Self-comparison is realised through the streak counter, the level bar, and the activity statistics history, all of which compare the student to their own past.

One response was deliberately not adopted: permanent full-table visibility is offered nowhere in the student-facing interface, because the evidence reviewed in Section 2.7 indicates that it concentrates motivational benefit at the top and concentrates discouragement at the bottom — an outcome incompatible with an intervention whose target population includes students who are currently least active (Alkhawaldeh et al., 2024).

### 2.7.2 The Trust Precondition: Ranking Only Motivates If It Is Believed

A frequently overlooked condition sits underneath every comparison mechanic: a ranking is only a source of information if the ranking is believed to be accurate. Social comparison theory describes the mechanism by which people use others as a standard for self-evaluation (Festinger, 1954), but the mechanism presupposes that the measured quantity is real. If awards can be obtained without effort — by photographing a friend's screen, by guessing codes, by running a modified client — then the leaderboard stops measuring fitness and starts measuring exploitation, and every student who notices withdraws the belief that the comparison is informative. The documented disengagement of low-ranked users is bad; the disengagement of *all* users when a ranking is perceived as farmable is terminal.

This establishes an argument that the thesis returns to in Chapter Five: the validation layer is not only a security component, it is a **motivation** component. Server-side computation, cooldowns, and the append-only ledger (Sections 2.6.1 and 4.5) are what make rank credible; the visible award message after each scan states the rule that produced the points; and the fact that an award is attributable to a named checkpoint gives the number a story a student can verify against their own memory. Perceived procedural fairness — the sense that the rules are known, applied uniformly, and auditable — is the bridge between the database transaction and the leaderboard's motivational effect.

The trust precondition also sets an evaluation target. A pilot should measure not only whether students scan, but whether they believe the ranking: a single question about confidence in the fairness of the leaderboard distinguishes a working comparison mechanic from a decorative one. Section 5.8's instrument includes open items capable of surfacing exactly this, and Section 6.7 recommends fairness review as an institutional policy.

## 2.8 Student Wellness in Higher Education

The population this system targets is unusually well documented. Edelmann et al. (2022) found that 22.4% of university students in their sample were insufficiently active, with higher inactivity among female and diverse-gender respondents. Alkhawaldeh et al. (2024) reported that fewer than half (48.1%) of surveyed students engaged in physical activity, with 41.4% classified as inactive, and identified perceived barriers including time pressure, lack of motivation, and absence of convenient facilities. Roberts et al. (2024) estimated that 37% of students failed to meet the 150-minute weekly moderate-to-vigorous activity guideline and 56% failed the muscle-strengthening guideline.

These figures matter for design in three ways. First, a substantial share of the target population is not merely unmotivated but *inactive to a degree that a beginner-facing entry point is required* — hence a campus of tiered checkpoints where the easiest reward requires walking to a building students already visit. Second, the barriers reported are structural as well as motivational: time, convenience, and intimidation. A system that uses space students already occupy removes the convenience barrier rather than exhorting them to overcome it. Third, inactivity correlates with sedentary academic routines, so the intervention must fit around timetables rather than compete with them; the cooldown-based, on-demand scan model does exactly that.

University wellness programmes are typically push-based: campaigns, gym inductions, and one-off events. The engagement decay documented for mobile health applications (Stawarz et al., 2015) suggests that push campaigns alone cannot sustain behaviour, which is why CampusFit is designed as an always-on system with continuous content (checkpoints, challenges, clubs) administered through a dashboard rather than as a campaign with a fixed end date.

### 2.8.1 Why Activity Matters for This Population: Mental Health and Academic Outcomes

The case for student physical activity is not limited to long-term disease risk, and a student audience is more responsive to proximal benefits than to distal ones. Biddle and Asare (2011), synthesising the review literature for children and adolescents, reported small but reasonably consistent associations between physical activity and lower depression and anxiety, higher self-esteem, and better cognitive functioning, while cautioning that the causal direction is difficult to establish from the available designs. Loprinzi (2019) supplied the academic link most relevant to this population: acute exercise produces measurable improvements in episodic memory function, which situates a walk between classes inside the study process rather than in competition with it. The World Health Organization (2020) guidelines set the quantitative target — adults should accumulate substantial moderate-intensity activity across the week and should also perform muscle-strengthening activity — a target that the reviewed student populations fail at scale (Roberts et al., 2024).

Three implications follow for this system's positioning. First, benefit framing can honestly emphasise immediate, academically adjacent returns (attention, memory, mood regulation) alongside the conventional long-term framing, because those are the outcomes students experience within a week rather than within decades. Second, the intervention should not medicalise activity: CampusFit positions itself as a wellness and participation system, records no clinical data, and makes no clinical claims — a boundary already enforced by the data-minimisation rule in Section 3.7. Third, because the mental-health associations are correlational in the underlying evidence, the thesis claims participation and engagement outcomes only; it does not claim mental-health improvement, and Chapter Six marks that boundary explicitly.

### 2.8.2 Barrier Taxonomy and Corresponding System Responses

The barriers documented in Section 2.8 are not a single phenomenon, and treating "laziness" as the explanation would be both empirically wrong and design-fatal. A taxonomy is useful because each barrier class demands a different response: some are removed by removing friction, others require motivation, and others require belonging. Table 2.3 states the mapping used in this study.

Table 2.3
*Student barriers and corresponding system responses*

| Barrier class | Evidence in the review | System response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time pressure | Schedules and competing academic demands reported as the leading barrier (Alkhawaldeh et al., 2024) | Opportunistic scans on routes already taken; sub-three-second core action; no gym session required |
| Low motivation and absent accountability | 41.4% classified as inactive (Alkhawaldeh et al., 2024); 37% miss the weekly guideline (Roberts et al., 2024) | Points, streaks, weekly league, club challenges — the motivation layer of Section 2.10 |
| Intimidation and facility access | Traditional gym and sport settings do not appeal to all students (Roberts et al., 2024) | No equipment, no changing rooms, no audience; the easiest award requires walking to a building already visited |
| Invisible progress | Self-monitoring without feedback underperforms monitoring with feedback (Patterson et al., 2021) | Progress ring, level ladder, distance-to-next-level text, activity statistics (Section 2.4.2) |
| Forgetting and failed habit | Logging-based applications decay (Stawarz et al., 2015); automaticity takes weeks (Lally et al., 2010) | Campus-embedded cues, streak counter, cooldown cadence (Section 2.2.5) |
| Solo activity without belonging | Relatedness is a basic psychological need (Ryan & Deci, 2000) | Clubs, crews, shared FitTrip routes, cooperative totals |

The mapping is a design hypothesis rather than a result: each response is justified by a technique, but whether it *works* for this population is exactly what the pilot in Section 5.8 is intended to test. Recording the mapping before data collection is what allows the pilot to falsify a specific response instead of producing an undifferentiated satisfaction score.

## 2.9 Comparative Review of Existing Systems

To locate the contribution precisely, this section compares representative systems across five dimensions: activity tracking, contextual integration with a specific physical environment, social competition, reward audibility, and institutional administration.

**Generalist fitness trackers (Strava, Nike Run Club, Google Fit).** These products provide excellent GPS and sensor-based tracking, rich history, and social feeds. Their weakness for this research problem is contextual: they measure effort anywhere, treat the environment as a blank map, and offer no mechanism through which an institution can seed specific, meaningful locations. They also depend on continuous sensor access, which has battery and privacy costs.

**Story-driven exercise games (Zombies Run, Habitica).** Zombies Run converts running into a narrative; Habitica role-plays productivity as an RPG. Both demonstrate that framing substantially changes persistence. Neither engages the user's actual campus geography, and Habitica's rewards are untethered from physical location.

**Location-based games (Pokémon Go and successors).** Althoff et al. (2016) and Li et al. (2021) established that these products measurably increase walking. Their relevance is direct: they prove that place-based collection motivates movement. Their limitation is that the places are arbitrary game content rather than the user's own institutions, that novelty decays, and that they are entertainment products rather than wellness interventions with auditable outcomes.

**Institutional and orientation scavenger hunts.** Universities routinely run QR-based hunts for induction weeks. The published literature on these is largely descriptive practice reporting rather than instrumented evaluation. Crucially, they are events with a start and an end; they do not maintain a persistent, auditable points economy for wellness.

**The gap.** No reviewed system combines (a) persistent campus-anchored checkpoints, (b) server-side auditable reward allocation with anti-abuse controls, (c) a competition layer designed against the documented demotivation risks of leaderboards, and (d) an institutional administration surface — in one system evaluated as a single artefact. CampusFit is that combination. Table A.1 in Appendix A restates this comparison in matrix form against the five dimensions.

### 2.9.1 Why Not Wearables or a Sensor-First Design

The most obvious alternative design would drop checkpoints entirely and rely on continuous sensing: a wrist-worn tracker or the phone's own motion coprocessor counting steps and awarding automatically. Wearable ecosystems (Fitbit, Apple Watch, Garmin and their peers) demonstrate that continuous self-monitoring is valuable — McCallum et al. (2018) found that such products dominate the evidence base while also noting that their evaluations rarely capture long-term engagement or contextual fit.

Four considerations ruled out a sensor-first architecture for this study, and each is a property of the *context* rather than of the sensors. **Equity of access.** A wrist device is a purchase; a phone camera is already owned by the student population assumed in Section 1.6, and requiring hardware would exclude precisely the students the intervention targets. **Consent and privacy.** Continuous accelerometry and heart-rate capture are biometric streams; the data-minimisation principle in Section 3.7 records that this system collects neither, and a checkpoint visit is a discrete, user-initiated act rather than a passive trace. **Attribution.** A sensor observes motion but cannot distinguish a walk to the library from a walk to the bus; a checkpoint award is attributable to a named place, which is what makes the leaderboard and the club challenges legible as stories rather than as numbers. **Battery and background limits.** A web-delivered application has constrained background sensing, as acknowledged in Section 3.5.1, so a sensor-first design would have forced a native architecture and abandoned the single-codebase portability requirement (NFR6).

This does not make sensing a wrong direction — it is recorded as future work in Section 6.8, where the checkpoint layer would remain as the place-anchor and sensors would supply effort rather than presence.

## 2.10 Research Gap and Conceptual Framework

The literature yields four converging observations. First, gamification reliably increases physical activity, but effectiveness depends on the combination of mechanics rather than on any single element (Hamari et al., 2014; Mazeas et al., 2022; Nishi et al., 2024). Second, location-based collection demonstrably converts geography into motivation (Althoff et al., 2016; Li et al., 2021), yet the effects fade without a continuing content supply. Third, rewards must satisfy competence, autonomy, and relatedness to avoid undermining intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Deci et al., 1999). Fourth, the student population most in need is characterised by structural barriers that a campus-anchored, opportunistic intervention is specifically suited to remove (Edelmann et al., 2022; Alkhawaldeh et al., 2024).

From these observations the conceptual framework of this study is derived. It has four layers:

1. **Physical layer** — checkpoints placed at existing campus locations, carrying QR codes (NFC-ready) that assert a visit.
2. **Validation layer** — a server-side routine that converts an assertion into an auditable award, enforcing cooldowns, difficulty multipliers, and append-only ledger entries.
3. **Motivation layer** — points, levels, streaks, badges, and a bounded weekly leaderboard, each mapped to a behaviour-change technique (Section 2.3.4) and constrained by SDT and flow considerations.
4. **Community and governance layer** — clubs, shared challenges, and an administrative dashboard that supplies the ongoing content the novelty-decay literature demands.

Table 2.2
*The conceptual framework: layers, techniques and research questions*

| Layer | Core elements | Primary techniques engaged | Questions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | Checkpoints, QR/NFC codes, printed signs | Action planning; restructuring the environment | RQ3 |
| Validation | `scan_checkpoint`, cooldown, append-only ledger | Contingent reward; monitoring; feedback | RQ2, RQ3, RQ5 |
| Motivation | Points, levels, badges, streaks, weekly leaderboard | Competence feedback; social comparison; habit formation | RQ1, RQ4 |
| Community and governance | Clubs, shared challenges, administration dashboard | Social support; identity; ongoing content supply | RQ1, RQ6 |

Each layer corresponds to specific research questions: layer 2 answers research questions 2, 3, and 5; layer 3 answers questions 1 and 4; layer 4 and the evaluation of the whole answer question 6. This correspondence is what allows Chapter Five to evaluate the study against its objectives rather than merely reporting that the software runs.

**Falsifiability of the framework.** A framework that cannot fail is not a research contribution, so the four layers are stated with the observable signatures that would count against them. If the physical layer works but habit does not form, scans will be front-loaded and then decay within the window — a retention curve, not a cadence — and the intervention would need content rather than cues. If the validation layer fails, the ledger will show patterns inconsistent with physical presence (rapid sequences, clustered redeems) and rankings will lose their trust precondition (Section 2.7.2). If the motivation layer misfires, the differential-response prediction of Section 2.3.6 will show up as bottom-ranked disengagement without compensating activity on the personal-progress path. And if the governance layer is insufficient, checkpoint and challenge content will go stale and decay will resume despite everything below it working. Each signature is measurable from the data the system already emits — the ledger, the scan timestamps, rank positions, and content ages — which is why Chapter Six can recommend longitudinal ledger analysis as the natural next study rather than as an aspiration.

**From framework to requirements.** The framework also does the practical work of converting theory into testable engineering obligations, which is the bridge to Chapter Three. The physical layer implies a non-functional requirement of legibility and immediacy: the sign must be readable and the core action must complete in seconds, or the prompt fails at the ability step of the behaviour model (Section 2.5.1). The validation layer implies the security requirement that no reward path may depend on client-supplied values (NFR3), and the availability requirement that the shell render without a network (NFR4), because an intervention whose cue is physical cannot be hostage to connectivity. The motivation layer implies the usability requirement (NFR1) — a reward surface the student understands without instruction — and the differential-response requirement that a non-competitive path exist at equal visual weight. The governance layer implies the scalability and portability requirements (NFR5, NFR6): ranking must remain responsive as the student table grows, and a single codebase must reach every device a student actually owns. Each of these obligations is stated formally in Section 3.3.3 and implemented in Chapter Four, which is the concrete sense in which the framework is conceptual rather than decorative: it does not merely explain the system, it specifies it.

## 2.11 Summary

This chapter has reviewed the theoretical foundations (self-determination, flow, habit formation, and behaviour-change frameworks), the empirical evidence for gamification in physical activity including its limits and dark sides, the motivational affordances of individual mechanics and the heterogeneity of response they produce, mobile fitness applications with their engagement-decay and attrition problems, location-based activity systems and their novelty-decay problem, persuasive technology and the behaviour model that explains prompt placement, QR and NFC validation media together with an explicit five-threat model, leaderboard design through social comparison theory, the specific condition of student physical activity and its mental-health and academic correlates, and the comparison of existing systems — including the sensor-first alternative — against five dimensions. It closed by stating the research gap and the four-layer conceptual framework that the methodology operationalises.

The review establishes that the research problem is neither "fitness apps do not exist" nor "gamification is unproven", but rather that no existing system unifies campus-anchored checkpoints, auditable server-side reward allocation, competition design informed by social comparison risks, and institutional governance. Chapter Three now turns to the methodology by which this claim is operationalised, built, and tested.
